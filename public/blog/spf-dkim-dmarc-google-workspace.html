How to Set Up SPF, DKIM, and DMARC for Google Workspace (2026 Guide)

By Steve Moynihan, Google Workspace Consultant · Updated Oct 30, 2026 · 14 min read

If your business runs on Google Workspace and you haven't configured SPF, DKIM, and DMARC, your email is exposed twice over: anyone can send mail that looks like it came from your domain, and your real invoices and proposals are more likely to land in spam. This guide gives you the exact DNS records for Google Workspace, the Admin Console steps, a safe DMARC rollout plan, and how to check that everything passes.

What Are SPF, DKIM, and DMARC?

Think of these three DNS records as a building's security desk. SPF is the guest list, DKIM is a tamper-proof seal on every package, and DMARC is the instruction card telling the guard what to do when someone shows up who isn't on the list.

  • →
    SPF (Sender Policy Framework): A TXT record listing the servers allowed to send email for your domain. Receiving servers check the sending server against that list.
  • →
    DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail): Google signs every outgoing message with a private key. You publish the matching public key in DNS so receivers can confirm the message really came from you and wasn't altered.
  • →
    DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting & Conformance): The policy layer. It tells receivers what to do when a message fails (nothing, spam folder, or reject) and sends you daily reports on who is sending as your domain.

The piece most guides skip is alignment. DMARC only passes when SPF or DKIM passes and the domain it checked matches the domain in the visible From: address. A newsletter tool that passes SPF for its own domain but sends From: you@yourcompany.com still fails DMARC unless it signs with DKIM for your domain. That's why third-party senders are where most setups break.

Real-world impact: Without DMARC, anyone can send email from your domain to your clients, and they won't know it's fake. Spoofed invoice and payment-change emails are one of the most common ways small businesses lose money and client trust.

Why This Is Now Mandatory

Since February 2024, Gmail and Yahoo require every sender to authenticate with SPF or DKIM. Anyone sending more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail must have SPF, DKIM, and a DMARC record, with the From: domain aligned. Microsoft applied similar rules to Outlook.com in 2025. Even if you send far less than that, mail without authentication is treated as suspicious, and Gmail shows recipients a "Be careful with this message" warning when it can't verify you.

Step 1: Set Up SPF

Sign in to wherever your domain's DNS lives (Cloudflare, GoDaddy, Namecheap, Squarespace Domains, etc.) and add one TXT record at the root of your domain:

// DNS TXT record — Google Workspace only
Host / Name: @
Value: v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all
TTL: 3600 (or Auto)

If other services also send email as your domain (a CRM, an email-marketing tool, a helpdesk, your accounting software), add each one's include: to the same record. Each vendor's help docs list the value to use:

// Example: Google Workspace plus a transactional email service
v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:sendgrid.net ~all

~all or -all?

~all (soft fail) marks unlisted senders as suspicious; -all (hard fail) tells receivers to reject them. Once DMARC is enforcing, DMARC makes the final call anyway, so ~all is a safe long-term choice. Use -all only when you're certain every legitimate sender is listed.

The two SPF rules that break everything

  • ✗ Only one SPF record per domain. Two records starting with v=spf1 make SPF fail for all your mail. Merge them.
  • ✗ Ten DNS lookups maximum. Every include:, a, mx, and nested include counts. Go over ten and SPF returns a permanent error. Remove services you no longer use, and prefer DKIM-based authentication for vendors that offer it.

Step 2: Turn On DKIM in Google Workspace

DKIM is off by default in Google Workspace. Until you turn it on, Google signs your mail with a generic Google key that doesn't align with your domain, which means it does nothing for DMARC.

  1. 1. Sign in to admin.google.com as a super admin.
  2. 2. Go to Apps → Google Workspace → Gmail → Authenticate email.
  3. 3. Pick your domain, then click Generate new record. Choose a 2048-bit key and leave the prefix selector as google.
  4. 4. Copy the TXT record name (google._domainkey) and value (it starts with v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=).
  5. 5. Add that TXT record at your DNS provider.
  6. 6. Wait for DNS to update (usually under an hour, up to 48 hours), then return to the same screen and click Start authentication. The status should change to "Authenticating email with DKIM."
// DNS TXT record — value shortened
Host / Name: google._domainkey
Value: v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIIBIjANBgkqhkiG9w0BAQEFAAOCAQ8AMIIBCgKCAQEA…

⚠️ Some older DNS panels cap a TXT value at 255 characters and silently cut a 2048-bit key in half. If Google says the record is invalid, check the saved value is complete. Most panels (including Cloudflare) split long values automatically; a few need you to paste it as two quoted strings.

Do the same for every service that sends as your domain. Most email-marketing and CRM tools give you one or two CNAME records for DKIM. Those are what make their mail align for DMARC, and they don't count against SPF's ten-lookup limit.

Step 3: Add a DMARC Policy

Start with a monitor-only policy. It changes nothing about delivery but starts the daily reports, so you can see every service sending as you before you enforce anything:

// DNS TXT record
Host / Name: _dmarc
Value: v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.com
TTL: 3600

What each tag means:

TagWhat it doesTypical value
pPolicy for your main domainnone → quarantine → reject
ruaWhere daily aggregate reports goA group or shared mailbox, not a person's inbox
pctShare of failing mail the policy applies to25, then 100 during rollout
spPolicy for subdomains (defaults to p)Same as p, or reject if you never send from subdomains
adkim / aspfStrict (s) or relaxed (r) alignmentLeave at the default, relaxed

⚠️ Aggregate reports are XML files, one per receiver per day. Create a Google Group or dedicated mailbox for them, and use a DMARC report viewer (many have free tiers) to turn them into readable charts.

A Safe DMARC Rollout Schedule

Jumping straight to p=reject is the fastest way to block your own invoices. Move up one step at a time, and only when the reports show every legitimate sender passing:

p=none
Weeks 1–4: Monitor
Read reports. Fix SPF/DKIM for every real sender you find.
p=quarantine; pct=25→100
Weeks 5–8: Quarantine
Failing mail goes to spam. Ramp pct up as reports stay clean.
p=reject
Week 9+: Reject
Spoofed mail is refused outright. Keep reading reports monthly.

For a small business with just Google Workspace and one or two other tools, the whole process often takes six to eight weeks. If the reports show nothing but Google and services you've already fixed, you can move faster.

How to Check It's Working

In Gmail: send a message from your domain to a personal Gmail account, open it, click the three-dot menu, and choose Show original. You want three lines reading PASS:

SPF: PASS with IP 209.85.220.41
DKIM: 'PASS' with domain yourdomain.com
DMARC: 'PASS'

The DKIM line must name your domain, not gappssmtp.com. If it shows Google's domain, DKIM isn't turned on yet (Step 2, item 6).

From a terminal: query the records directly to confirm what the world sees:

dig TXT yourdomain.com +short
dig TXT google._domainkey.yourdomain.com +short
dig TXT _dmarc.yourdomain.com +short

With Google's tools: the free Google Admin Toolbox Check MX flags missing or broken SPF, DKIM, and DMARC records. If you send marketing email, Google Postmaster Tools shows your domain's spam rate and authentication pass rates over time.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • ✗ Two SPF records: Very common after switching email providers. Delete the old one and merge its includes.
  • ✗ Publishing DKIM but never clicking Start authentication: The DNS record alone does nothing. Google has to start signing with it.
  • ✗ Jumping straight to p=reject: Always start with p=none and watch the reports first, or you will block legitimate email you forgot about.
  • ✗ Forgetting third-party senders: Your website's contact form, invoicing tool, and newsletter platform all send as you. Each needs SPF and, ideally, its own DKIM.
  • ✗ No rua address: Without reports you can't see who's sending as your domain, so you can never safely enforce.
  • ✗ Leaving parked domains unprotected: Domains you own but don't send from can still be spoofed. Give them v=spf1 -all and v=DMARC1; p=reject;.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Google Workspace set up SPF, DKIM, and DMARC automatically?

Usually not. Google can add some records for you if you bought the domain through Google during sign-up, but DKIM signing has to be started in the Admin Console, and no DMARC record is created for you. Check all three yourself.

How long do SPF, DKIM, and DMARC changes take to work?

Most DNS changes are visible within an hour, but allow up to 48 hours. DKIM only starts working after you click Start authentication in Google Admin, once the DNS record is visible.

Will setting up DMARC stop my emails from going to spam?

Missing or broken authentication is the most common reason legitimate business email lands in spam, so fixing it usually helps quickly. It won't fix a blocklisted IP, a poor sending reputation, or spammy content, but it's the foundation everything else depends on.

Should I use ~all or -all in my SPF record?

With DMARC in place, ~all is a safe long-term choice because DMARC decides what happens to failing mail. Use -all only when you're certain every service that sends as your domain is listed in the record.

Do I need DMARC if I'm a small business that sends very little email?

Yes. DMARC protects your domain from being impersonated, which matters regardless of how much you send. A monitoring-only record takes five minutes to add and starts showing you who is sending as your domain.
